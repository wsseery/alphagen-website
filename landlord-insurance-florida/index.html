Landlord and small apartment building insurance in Florida

A rental property needs a landlord policy, not a homeowners policy. Single-family rentals and condos are usually written on a dwelling fire form; small apartment buildings on a commercial habitational policy. Both answer different questions from the policy on the home you live in.

Who it's for

Owners of single-family rental homes, rented condo units, duplexes through small apartment buildings, and short-term rentals where the insurer allows them.

Owners whose lender requires proof of coverage on an investment property.

What it can include

  • The dwelling or building — on a named-peril or broader form, depending on the policy chosen.
  • Loss of rents — income lost while a covered loss makes the unit unrentable, on many forms.
  • Landlord liability — claims by tenants or visitors arising from the property's condition.
  • Wind — included or written separately, depending on the insurer and location.

Coverage depends on the policy. Nothing on this page describes what a specific policy will do: eligibility, underwriting and the terms, conditions and exclusions of the policy actually issued decide that.

What insurers ask for

To quote this line, an underwriter needs a picture of the risk. These are the items most insurers ask for. Not every insurer asks for every item, and some ask for more.

Owner and occupancy

  • Who owns the property (you or an entity) and how it is rented: annual leases, short-term, or vacant — short-term and vacant units carry more liability and water-damage claims, and some insurers will not write them
  • Number of units and whether you live in one of them

Property details

  • Construction type, year built, square footage and number of stories
  • Roof type and age, and updates to the roof, wiring, plumbing and heating and cooling
  • Fire protection: sprinklers, alarms and the local protection class
  • Wind-mitigation features such as roof-to-wall connections and opening protection — in Florida these drive much of the wind premium, and Florida requires insurers to offer credits for them
  • Flood zone
  • A four-point inspection for older homes, and a roof inspection where the insurer asks for one
  • Pools, docks and other features that add liability

History

  • Three to five years of currently valued loss runs from your current and prior insurers — they show claims the insurer would otherwise only learn about later
  • Your current insurer, current premium and expiration date

Application forms: most insurers start from the industry-standard ACORD 84 Dwelling Fire Application for one- to four-family homes, or the ACORD 125 and ACORD 140 for larger buildings; many also use their own application or supplement.

Having your loss runs, inspections and current declarations ready usually speeds up a quote, because the underwriter can price from documents instead of waiting on follow-up questions.

Questions we hear

Can I keep my homeowners policy when I rent the house out?

Usually not. A homeowners policy assumes you live there. Once the home is rented, insurers generally expect a landlord or dwelling fire policy instead.

Does my tenant's renters policy protect the building?

No. A renters policy insures the tenant's belongings and liability, not your building.

Do short-term rentals need different insurance?

Often yes. Many landlord policies exclude or limit short-term rentals, so tell the insurer how the property is rented.

How much does landlord insurance cost in Florida?

What drives the price is the property itself: its location and flood zone, its age, roof and construction, its wind-mitigation features, the coverage limits and deductibles chosen, and its claims history.
