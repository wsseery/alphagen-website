Surety bonds in Florida

A surety bond is a promise made by a surety company to the party that requires the bond (the obligee) that your business (the principal) will meet an obligation, such as finishing a contract or following the rules of a license. It is not insurance for your business: if the surety pays a claim, you are expected to pay it back.

Who it's for

Contractors on public work. Florida requires a payment and performance bond on contracts to build or repair public buildings and public works (Florida Statutes section 255.05).

Contractors whose private project owners or lenders ask for bid, performance or payment bonds.

Businesses asked for a license or permit bond by a state agency or a local government.

What it can include

  • Bid bonds — that you will sign the contract and provide the final bonds if your bid is accepted.
  • Performance bonds — that you will complete the contract according to its terms.
  • Payment bonds — that your subcontractors and suppliers will be paid.
  • License and permit bonds — that you will follow the laws and rules tied to a license or permit.

Coverage depends on the policy. Nothing on this page describes what a specific policy will do: eligibility, underwriting and the terms, conditions and exclusions of the policy actually issued decide that.

What insurers ask for

To quote this line, an underwriter needs a picture of the risk. These are the items most insurers ask for. Not every insurer asks for every item, and some ask for more.

The bond

  • The bond type, the bond amount and who requires it (the obligee)
  • A copy of the bond form, or the contract, license or permit requirement that calls for the bond

Financials, for contract bonds

  • Business financial statements, prepared by a certified public accountant for larger bond programs
  • A work-in-progress schedule: contracts underway, their value and how far along each one is — it shows how much unfinished work the surety would already be backing
  • Your bank line of credit and bank reference
  • Owners' personal financial statements and consent to a credit check — owners usually sign the indemnity agreement personally

Experience and history

  • Your largest completed projects and the experience of your key people
  • Any past bond claims, contract defaults or unfinished projects

Application forms: there is no common ACORD form for surety bonds; each surety company uses its own application, and contract bonds also need financial statements.

Having your loss runs, inspections and current declarations ready usually speeds up a quote, because the underwriter can price from documents instead of waiting on follow-up questions.

Questions we hear

Is a surety bond the same as insurance?

No. A bond protects the obligee, not your business. Before issuing a bond, the surety usually has the business, and often its owners, sign an indemnity agreement promising to repay any claim it pays.

What is bonding capacity?

The size of a single contract and the total of all bonded work a surety will support for you. It is set from your financial statements, your experience and the work you already have underway.

Why does the surety want my personal financial information?

For most small and mid-sized businesses, the owners personally sign the indemnity agreement, so the surety looks at their finances and credit as well as the company's.

How long does it take to get a bond?

Many license and permit bonds can be issued quickly from a short application. Contract bonds take longer, because the surety reviews your financial statements and work in progress first.

What drives the price of a bond?

The type and amount of the bond, the financial strength and credit of the business and its owners, and your experience with similar work.
