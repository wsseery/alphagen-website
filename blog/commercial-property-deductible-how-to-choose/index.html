Your commercial property deductible: how to choose between the premium and the reserve

Year-end is when a Broward County building owner or association board actually has the numbers in front of them — the reserve balance, next year’s premium quote, and the gap between the two. That is the right moment to look hard at the deductible, because a commercial property deductible is not an insurance decision. It is a decision about how much of your own cash you are willing to put in front of the policy, and it is the only line on the quote you can move in either direction.

What actually happens at renewal

Illustrative example. The businesses in this post, and the figures and timelines that describe them, are hypothetical, used to show how the rules apply. They are not statistics and not real clients. Statute figures are cited to their source.

An association gets two options at renewal. Option A carries a $25,000 all-other-perils deductible at a $48,000 premium. Option B carries a $100,000 deductible at a $39,500 premium. The board looks at an $8,500 saving and takes Option B, because $8,500 is real money and a claim is hypothetical.

Then a pipe fails in March and the repair comes to $61,000. Under Option B the association pays all of it and the policy pays nothing, because the loss never reached the deductible. One year’s saving bought a $61,000 special assessment.

That is not an argument for the lowest deductible available. It is an argument for choosing the number against a reserve balance rather than against a premium quote.

What the rules actually require here

Two parts of Florida law shape what you will be offered, and both are worth knowing before the conversation.

First, how the deductible is written. Unless the office determines the deductible provision is clear and unambiguous, Florida provides that “a property insurer may not issue an insurance policy or contract covering real property in this state which contains a deductible provision that” — among other things — “States the deductible as a percentage rather than as a specific amount of money,” “Applies solely to hurricane losses,” or “Applies solely to a roof loss” (Fla. Stat. §627.701(2)(a)–(c), 2026 Florida Statutes). That rule is written for policies covering real property in this state generally, not for one line of business.

Second, if the building is commercial residential — a condominium association, a cooperative, an apartment building — the statute provides that “for commercial residential property insurance policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2006, the insurer must offer the policyholder the following alternative hurricane deductibles” (Fla. Stat. §627.701(5)(b), 2026 Florida Statutes). Alternatives have to be offered to you. They do not arrive chosen.

Most commercial property policies carry two separate deductibles — one for hurricane or windstorm losses, one for everything else — and the two rarely want the same answer. The all-other-perils deductible pays for pipes, fires and vandalism. The hurricane deductible decides what the building does after a named storm. Your own declarations page states both, and is where to read them side by side.

Where the gap usually sits

The gap is almost always that the deductible was chosen against the premium and never revisited against the reserve.

A $100,000 deductible is sound for an owner who keeps $250,000 liquid and poor for an association whose reserve is committed to a roof project in Q2. Nothing about the policy changes between those two owners. What changes is whether the money is reachable in the week the loss happens — not after an assessment vote, not after a loan.

The second gap is treating the two deductibles as one decision. Boards often accept a high hurricane deductible, defensible in Florida, and then let the all-other-perils number drift up with it, which usually is not.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Write down both deductibles from your declarations page — the hurricane or windstorm deductible and the all-other-perils deductible — as specific dollar amounts.
  2. Put your reachable cash next to them. Not total reserves: the amount you could spend within a week without a vote, a loan or a special assessment.
  3. Ask your agent to quote the alternatives. If the building is commercial residential, alternative hurricane deductibles have to be offered to you; ask for the premium at each one in writing.
  4. Price the saving against one loss, not one year. Take the premium difference between two options and divide the deductible difference by it. That is how many claim-free years the higher deductible needs to break even.
  5. Check what else moves with the deductible. Ask whether the quoted options change any sub-limit, coinsurance provision or roof-loss settlement terms, and get the answer on the quote rather than in conversation.

Multifamily and HOA insurance in Broward County

For multifamily buildings and associations in Broward County, the deductible is the number most likely to turn an insured loss into a special assessment — and the one easiest to revisit while next year’s budget is still open. It is worth setting it against the reserve you will actually be holding in June, not the one on today’s statement.
