What does a Florida restaurant’s general liability policy actually exclude?

A Boca Raton owner renews general liability every year, files the certificate the landlord asked for, and never opens the policy again. Then a guest goes down near the dish pit, an argument in the parking lot turns physical at 1 a.m., or a county inspector calls about a batch of ceviche. That is usually the first time anyone reads the document properly — under pressure, with a lawyer on the phone. All three of those situations are decided on the exclusions page, and the exclusions page is the one nobody reads.

What actually happens

Three situations come up again and again in Palm Beach and Broward restaurants, and they are not variations of one problem — they are three different policies.

The first is the wet floor. A server drops a tray by the pass, someone steps in it four minutes later, and there is a claim. Under Florida law the injured person has to prove the restaurant had actual or constructive knowledge of the substance. Constructive knowledge can be shown, the statute says, where the condition “existed for such a length of time that, in the exercise of ordinary care, the business establishment should have known of the condition” or where it “occurred with regularity and was therefore foreseeable” (Fla. Stat. §768.0755). That second clause is why a sloppy sweep log is worse than no sweep log: a pattern you never fixed is evidence against you.

The second is the bar. Florida limits the liability of a business that serves alcohol to an adult — but not absolutely. The statute holds a seller liable where it “willfully and unlawfully sells or furnishes alcoholic beverages to a person who is not of lawful drinking age,” or where it “knowingly serves a person habitually addicted” (Fla. Stat. §768.125). Two narrow doors, both of which turn on what your staff knew and what you can show you trained them to do.

The third is the food itself. The CDC’s 2019 estimates put domestically acquired foodborne illness from seven major pathogens at roughly 53,300 hospitalizations and 931 deaths a year, with about 9.9 million illnesses attributed to six of them (CDC, Burden of Foodborne Illness). A single one of those landing on your dining room is a third-party claim, a health-department file and a week of local news, in that order.

What a general liability policy does here

Spell the acronym out once, because it matters: a commercial general liability (CGL) policy is built around claims brought by other people against your business. Most CGL forms are organised into a bodily injury and property damage part, a personal and advertising injury part, and a small medical payments part — and most restaurant policies also carry a products-completed operations component, which is the part food-related claims are normally evaluated under. Most forms also fund the defence of a covered claim, which is frequently worth more than the settlement.

What it is not built around is your own losses. Damage to your walk-in, the value of the food inside it, and injuries to your own employees are all different products. That is the single most useful sentence in this post: general liability is third-party insurance, and a restaurant’s most expensive bad weeks are usually first-party events.

Whether any of this responds to a specific claim depends entirely on the terms of the policy actually issued to you. Read yours.

Where the gap usually sits

Four places, in roughly the order they surprise people.

Liquor. Most CGL forms carry a liquor liability exclusion that applies to businesses in the business of serving alcohol — which is to say, it applies to you specifically because you have a licence. The exposure described in §768.125 is normally addressed by a separate liquor liability policy, not by the GL you already bought. If you serve and you have not seen a second policy, that is a conversation to have this month.

Assault and battery. Restaurants and bars with late service are often written with an assault and battery exclusion or a sub-limit added by endorsement, and the sub-limit is frequently far below the policy’s headline limit. Owners rarely know which of the two they have.

Your own property and your own product. Spoilage after a compressor fails, the cost of the product you had to dump, the equipment itself — none of that is a third-party claim. It lives on a property policy, usually needing an equipment breakdown or spoilage endorsement to reach it at all.

Employee injury. Workers’ compensation, not general liability. Every year somebody finds this out the hard way.

What to check on your own policy

Five checks. Twenty minutes with the actual document, not the certificate.

  1. Find the exclusions page and read it start to finish. It is usually three to six pages and it is the only part of the policy that tells you what you have not bought.
  2. Look for the liquor liability exclusion — then confirm whether a separate liquor liability policy exists, and what its limit is. If you serve alcohol and cannot find a second policy number, stop and ask.
  3. Look for an assault and battery endorsement. Establish which it is: excluded outright, or sub-limited — and if sub-limited, to what number.
  4. Check the products-completed operations aggregate. Note whether it is a separate aggregate or shares the general aggregate, because a foodborne-illness claim and a slip-and-fall claim drawing on the same pot is a different risk than two pots.
  5. Check whether spoilage and equipment breakdown appear on your property policy at all. Many small restaurant packages leave both off by default.

Write down what you find. A renewal conversation goes very differently when you arrive with four answers instead of four questions.

Restaurant insurance in Boca Raton

Palm Beach County’s restaurant density along Glades Road, Mizner Park and the Delray corridor means two things for coverage: late-night alcohol service is common, so the liquor and assault-and-battery questions are live for more operators here than the statewide average would suggest, and hurricane-season business interruption sits underneath all of it from June through November. A Boca Raton restaurant’s insurance problem is rarely one policy — it is the seam between four of them. That is the review worth doing before your next renewal date, not after it.
