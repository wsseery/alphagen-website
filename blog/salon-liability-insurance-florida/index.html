Salon Liability Insurance in Florida: Three Risks Every Salon Owner Insures Too Late

It is common for a salon owner to buy general liability the week the lease is signed and not open the policy again. Salon liability insurance in Florida tends to come apart in three places, usually discovered after a claim: a client who reacts to a treatment, a booth renter whose work lands on the salon’s name, and the retail shelf by the front desk. All three sit in wording most owners have never read.

What actually happens — one salon, three questions

Illustrative example. The businesses in this post, and the figures and timelines that describe them, are hypothetical, used to show how the rules apply. They are not statistics and not real clients. Statute figures are cited to their source.

The salon is in Delray Beach. The owner employs two stylists, rents three chairs to independent stylists, and sells shampoo, color care and skin care at the front desk — including a small line with the salon’s own name on the label.

A long-time color client has a reaction to a service. Within a few weeks, three questions arrive. Does the general liability policy respond to an injury from a service, rather than a wet floor? Was the stylist an employee, a renter, or — to the client — simply “the salon”? And the client also used the salon’s own-label aftercare at home. Whose product is that?

Risk 1: the treatment reaction — general liability vs professional liability

Commercial general liability (CGL) is written for bodily injury and property damage arising from your premises and operations — a client slipping on the way to the shampoo bowl. A reaction to the service itself is a different question. Many general liability policies carry a professional services exclusion, which is “commonly endorsed onto general liability policies” and serves “to segregate general liability from professional (errors and omissions (E&O)) exposures, leaving only the former insured.” (IRMI, “professional services exclusion”) For a salon, that line runs straight through the chair.

The label itself flags the risk. Federal law requires coal-tar hair dyes to carry a caution statement that the product “contains ingredients which may cause skin irritation on certain individuals and a preliminary test according to accompanying directions should first be made.” (FD&C Act §601(a), 21 U.S.C. §361(a))

The federal definition of a serious cosmetic adverse event names exactly what salon owners worry about: “second- or third-degree burns, significant hair loss, or persistent or significant alteration of appearance.” (FD&C Act §604, 21 U.S.C. §364, added by the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 (MoCRA))

There is a trap on the other side too. “We bought professional liability” is not automatically the answer: “most professional liability policies only cover economic or financial losses suffered by third parties, as opposed to bodily injury (BI) and property damage (PD) claims.” (IRMI, “professional liability”) A chemical burn is bodily injury. What matters is whether the form you hold contemplates bodily injury arising out of your services — answered on the policy’s own pages, not by its name.

Risk 2: booth renters — whose policy answers?

In Florida, “cosmetology services shall be performed only by licensed cosmetologists in licensed salons” (Fla. Stat. §477.0263(1)), and no salon may operate without a license from the state (Fla. Stat. §477.025(1)). So every renter works inside your licensed salon, under your sign — and the client sees your name on the door, not the renter’s.

Whether a renter really runs their own business is a legal and tax question with more than one test:

  • The IRS describes a booth renter as someone who “leases space from an existing business and operates their own business as an independent contractor.” Its indications include a key, your own hours, your own products, your own phone number and business name, and setting your own prices; “if these factors are not present, then you are likely an employee of the business who is providing the space.” (IRS Publication 4902, Tax Tips for the Cosmetology & Barber Industry) The IRS’s general test weighs behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship. (IRS, “Independent contractor (self-employed) or employee?”)
  • Florida workers’ compensation uses its own list. Outside construction, an independent contractor must meet at least four of six criteria — among them a separate business with its own facility or equipment, a federal employer identification number, pay that goes to a business rather than an individual, and freedom to work for others (Fla. Stat. §440.02(18)(d)1.a). And “an individual claiming to be an independent contractor has the burden of proving” it (same section, 1.c).

The count matters. Outside construction, Florida’s workers’ compensation law reaches private employment “in which four or more employees are employed by the same employer” (Fla. Stat. §440.02(20)(b)2). In the example above, two employees plus three renters is five people if the renters turn out to be employees.

How your renters are classified is a question for your attorney and your accountant, not your insurance agent. What insurance can do is make sure the paperwork matches the arrangement:

  • A certificate of insurance from every renter. A certificate is “a document providing evidence that certain general types of insurance coverages and limits have been purchased” (IRMI). It is evidence of a policy, not the policy, and it has an expiration date.
  • Additional insured status. That is a person or organization “not automatically included as an insured” who is added to the policy “at the request of the named insured” (IRMI). Whether your rental agreement should require it is a question for your attorney.

Risk 3: the retail shelf — products-completed operations

Selling shampoo makes the salon a seller of a physical product. In insurance terms, that is the products-completed operations hazard, “one of the hazards ordinarily insured by a general liability policy,” which “encompasses liability arising out of the insured’s products.” (IRMI, “products-completed operations”) “Ordinarily” is the operative word. General liability declarations usually show it with its own aggregate limit, and an endorsement can limit or remove it.

The own-label line deserves a closer look. Under MoCRA, the “responsible person” for a cosmetic is “the manufacturer, packer, or distributor of a cosmetic product whose name appears on the label,” and the responsible person must report a serious adverse event to FDA “no later than 15 business days after the report is received.” (21 U.S.C. §364; 21 U.S.C. §364a(b)(1)) If your salon’s name is on the label, ask your attorney whether that makes you the responsible person.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Find the professional services exclusion in your general liability endorsements. If services are excluded, identify which policy, if any, addresses injury from your services — and confirm it addresses bodily injury, not only financial loss.
  2. If you carry professional liability, check the trigger. “The vast majority of professional liability policies are written with claims-made coverage triggers” (IRMI). On a claims-made form, confirm the retroactive date reaches back to when you started performing services.
  3. Collect a current certificate of insurance from every booth renter and calendar the expiration dates. Ask your attorney whether the rental agreement should require additional insured status, and your accountant how the IRS and Florida tests apply.
  4. Verify licenses against services. Look up each stylist, renter and specialist on the Florida DBPR license search and confirm the license or specialty registration (Fla. Stat. §477.013(6)) matches the services they perform.
  5. Find the products-completed operations aggregate on your declarations page, check for any endorsement that limits it, and list every product on your shelf whose label carries your salon’s name.

Nothing here states what any policy will pay. Coverage is determined by the terms, conditions and exclusions of the policy issued to you, and two policies with the same name can answer these three questions very differently.
