Professional liability insurance in Boca Raton: the claims general liability was never built for

Professional liability insurance in Boca Raton gets bought late, usually right after a service business discovers what its general liability policy is actually for. General liability answers for the physical world — someone slips, something gets broken. A service business’s real exposure is the advice it gave, the treatment it performed, or the result it promised and did not deliver. Those are different claims, and they are handled by a different policy.

What actually happens

A client is unhappy with an outcome, not an accident.

A spa performs a chemical peel and the client has a reaction that takes weeks to settle. A bookkeeper misses a filing date. A consultant’s recommendation costs the client a contract. In every case nobody fell over, nothing caught fire, and there is no property damage to point at. The complaint is that the work itself was done badly.

That is the line general liability does not cross. A general liability policy responds to bodily injury and property damage arising out of your premises and operations. An allegation that your professional judgement was wrong is, in insurance terms, an economic loss caused by rendering a service — and most general liability policies carry a professional services exclusion that says so explicitly. Check your exclusions page; it is usually one of the endorsements rather than the main form.

What professional liability insurance in Boca Raton does here

Professional liability — also sold as errors and omissions (E&O), and as malpractice in the licensed health and legal trades — responds to claims that the service was performed negligently, or not performed at all.

Two mechanics are worth understanding before you compare quotes.

It is almost always claims-made. General liability is usually occurrence-based: what matters is when the incident happened. Professional liability is usually claims-made: what matters is when the claim is made against you, and whether your policy was in force both then and when the work was done. That is what the retroactive date on the declarations page is for, and it is the single most expensive thing to get wrong when switching carriers.

Defence costs are the main event. Most professional liability policies address the cost of defending the allegation as well as any settlement — and on a service-business claim the defence is frequently the larger number, because establishing that the work was reasonable takes expert time. Whether those costs sit inside the limit or outside it changes what the limit is worth.

Florida timing matters here too. Under the state’s limitations statute, an action for professional malpractice other than medical malpractice — whether founded on contract or tort — must be commenced within two years, and the statute provides that the period “shall run from the time the cause of action is discovered or should have been discovered with the exercise of due diligence.” It also limits those actions to persons in privity with the professional. That is Fla. Stat. §95.11(5)(b), as published in the 2024 Florida Statutes (including 2025C).

Two years from discovery, not from the work, is why a claims-made policy and its retroactive date matter so much: the complaint can arrive long after the invoice was paid, and the policy that has to answer for it is the one in force when the complaint lands.

Where the gap usually sits

Booth renters and contractors are frequently nobody’s insured. A salon, spa or studio that rents chairs or rooms to independent operators often assumes the shop’s own policy answers for their work. It generally does not — their professional acts are theirs. Each renter needs their own professional liability, and the shop needs it evidenced, not assumed.

The named insured is too narrow. If the business operates as two entities, or added a trading name, or brought in a second practitioner, a policy still naming only the original entity will not follow the work.

There is no gap cover between policies. Let a claims-made policy lapse and the tail disappears with it. Extended reporting period — “tail” coverage — has to be bought, usually within a short window after cancellation.

General liability was assumed to be a bundle. A business owner’s policy (BOP) bundles general liability and property. It does not ordinarily include professional liability, and the absence is easy to miss because the certificate looks complete.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Find the retroactive date on the declarations page. If it is the same as the policy start date, work you did before that date is not in scope.
  2. Confirm whether defence costs are inside or outside the limit of liability.
  3. Read the named insured line against every entity and trading name the business actually uses.
  4. Check whether independent operators, booth renters or subcontractors are insureds — and if not, collect their certificates.
  5. Ask what the extended reporting period costs and how long you have to elect it, before you need to know.

Law firms, accountants and the offices next door

A professional office carries almost no inventory and almost all of its risk in its files, its advice and its email. For a law firm in particular, the exposure is the engagement itself — the missed deadline, the conflict nobody flagged, the advice that turned out to be wrong — which is why the coverage conversation looks nothing like a restaurant’s.
