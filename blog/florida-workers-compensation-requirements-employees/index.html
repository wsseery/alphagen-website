Florida Workers’ Compensation Requirements: Employees, Subcontractors, and the Count That Triggers Coverage

A Fort Lauderdale drywall contractor hires his first helper on a Monday morning. By Florida law, something changed that afternoon. A Boca Raton accounting firm can hire three people and nothing changes at all. Florida workers compensation requirements for employees are not one threshold — they are three, and which one applies turns on what the state calls your industry, not on how big your payroll feels. Here is how the count actually works in Broward and Palm Beach County.

What actually happens — the scenario, specifically

The drywall contractor has been a one-man operation for two years. He brings on a helper at $22 an hour, cash-flow permitting, with every intention of making it official once the next two jobs close.

Six weeks in, the helper falls off a four-foot bench and breaks his wrist. The emergency room asks who is paying. There is no carrier to name.

What happens next is not primarily an insurance question — it is a compliance question, and Florida answers it in statute. The contractor was required to secure coverage from the day the helper started, because in Florida the construction industry is counted differently from everything else.

Florida workers compensation requirements by employee count

Three thresholds, all in the same definition section of Chapter 440:

  • Construction industry — one or more employees. The statute covers “all private employment in which one or more employees are employed by the same employer.” (Fla. Stat. §440.02(17)(b))
  • Every other private employer — four or more employees. “All private employments in which four or more employees are employed by the same employer.” (same section)
  • Agricultural labor — more than five regular employees, or twelve or more seasonal workers for more than 30 days. (same section, subsection (c))

“Construction industry” is defined broadly, and this is where owners get caught out. It means for-profit activities involving building, clearing, filling, excavation, or substantial improvement in the size or use of a structure or the appearance of land. (Fla. Stat. §440.02) Landscaping that regrades a lot, a handyman who takes out a load-bearing wall, a solar installer on a roof — the label on the truck is not what decides it.

The officer exemption is narrower than most people think. A corporate officer may elect to be exempt, but in construction no more than three officers of a corporation or affiliated group may do so, and each must own at least ten percent of the stock. (Fla. Stat. §440.02(15)(b)) The certificate costs $50 and is valid for two years from its effective date. (Fla. Stat. §440.05) An exemption covers the officer. It does not cover a helper.

What non-compliance costs. The Division of Workers’ Compensation may serve a stop-work order “requiring the cessation of all business operations.” (Fla. Stat. §440.107(7)(a)) The penalty is two times the premium the employer would have paid on the unreported payroll during the period of non-compliance in the preceding 12 months — 24 months for a repeat violator or an employer who concealed payroll — or $1,000, whichever is greater. (Fla. Stat. §440.107(7)(d)1)

Read that twice. The penalty is calculated on premium that was never paid, doubled, looking backward. It is not a flat fine, and it grows with the payroll it was hiding.

Where the gap usually sits — the subcontractor you did not insure

The thresholds above are the part people eventually learn. The part that surprises general contractors with clean policies of their own is the statutory pass-through.

“In case a contractor sublets any part or parts of his or her contract work to a subcontractor or subcontractors, all of the employees of such contractor and subcontractor or subcontractors engaged on such contract work shall be deemed to be employed in one and the same business or establishment, and the contractor shall be liable for, and shall secure, the payment of compensation to all such employees, except to employees of a subcontractor who has secured such payment.” — Fla. Stat. §440.10(1)(b)

The exception is the whole sentence. If your sub secured coverage, their employees are theirs. If the sub did not, those employees are deemed to be yours — and the obligation to secure payment lands on you.

The same section tells you what to do about it: “A contractor shall require a subcontractor to provide evidence of workers’ compensation insurance,” and a subcontractor whose officer has elected exemption must hand over a copy of the exemption certificate. (Fla. Stat. §440.10(1)(c))

So the gap is almost never a policy wording problem. It is a paperwork problem. A certificate collected at bid time and never refreshed, a sub who let coverage lapse in month four of a nine-month job, an exemption certificate that expired two years after its effective date and was never replaced — each of those pushes an uninsured crew onto the general contractor’s experience, and onto the general contractor’s audit.

An audit looks backward at payroll. Uncertificated subcontractor payroll is routinely reclassified as the contractor’s own, at the contractor’s own class rate, and that is the bill most trade owners have never budgeted for.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Confirm which threshold applies to you. Is any part of what you do building, clearing, filling, excavating, or substantially improving a structure or land? If yes, the construction threshold of one employee is the one that governs — not four.
  2. Check whether anyone you treat as an exempt officer actually qualifies. Three officers maximum in construction, ten percent ownership each, and a certificate dated within the last two years.
  3. Pull every subcontractor certificate on every open job and check the expiration date, not just the file. A certificate proves what was true the day it was issued.
  4. Ask what your policy’s audit basis is and how uncertificated subcontractor payroll is treated. Most workers’ compensation policies address subcontractor payroll in their audit provisions — the premium-basis and audit sections of your own policy are where to confirm how yours does it.
  5. Ask when your next audit falls and what payroll records it will want. The time to find a missing certificate is before the auditor does.

Nothing above is a statement about what any particular policy will pay. Coverage, eligibility and exclusions are determined by the policy actually issued to you — the statutes are the floor, not the contract.

Contractor insurance in Fort Lauderdale

Broward and Palm Beach trade contractors run into the same three things: a one-employee threshold that arrives earlier than expected, an officer exemption that covers less than assumed, and subcontractor certificates that age out mid-job. None of those is exotic. All of them are cheaper to find in a file review than at an audit or a stop-work order.

If you work across both counties, or you add and release crew with the season, that is worth a conversation before the next renewal rather than after it.
