Commercial Umbrella Insurance: When the Extra Layer Is Actually Needed

A Broward County electrical contractor wins a shot at a hotel renovation and the general contractor’s insurance exhibit asks for $5 million. He has $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate. Commercial umbrella insurance is needed here for a reason that has nothing to do with how risky his work is — the contract decided it. That is the most common trigger, and it is worth separating from the other one, which is quieter and matters more.

Illustrative example. The businesses in this post, and the figures and timelines that describe them, are hypothetical, used to show how the rules apply. They are not statistics and not real clients. Statute figures are cited to their source.

What actually happens — the scenario, specifically

Two different owners buy an umbrella in the same month for two different reasons.

The first is the electrician above. He cannot bid without the limit. The umbrella is a cost of entry, priced against a job he has already won on everything but paper.

The second is a Fort Lauderdale landscaping company with eight trucks and a crew that works residential streets all day. Nobody has asked them for anything. Their primary general liability limit is $1 million per occurrence, which was a reasonable number when they had two trucks and no highway work.

The first owner knows exactly what he is buying. The second one is the one who usually finds out late.

What a commercial umbrella does here — the mechanism

An umbrella is not a broader policy. It is a higher one, and it is higher only over the specific policies listed on it.

Most commercial umbrella policies are written to respond above the underlying policies scheduled on them — typically general liability, commercial auto, and employers’ liability — once the underlying limit for that claim is exhausted. Which policies are scheduled, and the limit each must carry for the umbrella to sit on top of it, is set out on your umbrella’s schedule of underlying insurance. That schedule is the document to read, not the declarations page.

Two consequences follow, and both are where owners get surprised:

  • A line that is not on the schedule is not underneath the umbrella. Professional liability, cyber and pollution are commonly outside it unless specifically added.
  • If the underlying limit drops below what the schedule requires, the gap is not automatically filled. Most umbrella forms address this in a “maintenance of underlying insurance” condition — your own umbrella’s conditions section is where to confirm how yours handles it.

Where the gap usually sits — Florida’s fault rules put the primary limit in play

Here is the quieter reason, and it is structural rather than anecdotal.

Florida applies modified comparative negligence. Since the 2023 amendments, “any party found to be greater than 50 percent at fault for his or her own harm may not recover any damages” (Fla. Stat. §768.81(6)), and below that bar, “contributory fault chargeable to the claimant diminishes proportionately the amount awarded” (Fla. Stat. §768.81(2)).

Read that from the defendant’s side. For example, a claimant who is 40 percent at fault still recovers 60 percent of the award. On a serious injury, 60 percent of the number is frequently larger than a $1 million per-occurrence limit — and the defence of the claim runs through that same limit on many general liability forms.

The second structural pressure is contractual. Florida limits how far indemnification can be pushed in construction contracts, but it does so by requiring a number. An indemnification provision must contain “a monetary limitation on the extent of the indemnification that bears a reasonable commercial relationship to the contract,” and where a property owner is the party indemnified, that limitation “shall not be less than $1 million per occurrence, unless otherwise agreed by the parties.” (Fla. Stat. §725.06(1))

So $1 million is the statutory floor for that one scenario, not a target. Any contract written above it — and most institutional work is — is asking you to stand behind an obligation your primary limit was never sized for.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Read the schedule of underlying insurance, line by line. Confirm every policy you expect the umbrella to sit over is actually listed, with the limit it requires.
  2. Compare each scheduled requirement against the current limit on that underlying policy. A renewal that moved a limit can quietly put you out of step with your own umbrella.
  3. Pull the insurance exhibit from your three largest contracts and write down the limit each demands. Then compare it to your total primary plus umbrella.
  4. Ask whether defence costs erode your primary limit or sit outside it. This is a wording question on your own general liability form, and it changes how fast the umbrella is reached.
  5. Ask which lines are outside the umbrella entirely. Professional liability and cyber are the usual answers; confirm yours rather than assume.

None of the above says what any policy will pay. Coverage is determined by the terms, conditions and exclusions of the policy issued to you.

Contractor insurance in Fort Lauderdale

Trade contractors in Broward and Palm Beach hit the contract trigger first and the exposure trigger second, usually in that order and usually years apart. The useful time to size an umbrella is while you are reading a contract you have not signed, not while you are reading a demand letter.
