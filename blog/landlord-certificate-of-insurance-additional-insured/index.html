Landlord certificate of insurance additional insured: why it is not your coverage

A landlord certificate of insurance additional insured line looks reassuring, and for a tenant it is easy to read as “we are covered.” It is worth slowing down on that. A certificate is a document about your landlord’s policy, not a policy of your own.

What “additional insured” means

IRMI, the insurance-industry reference publisher, describes an additional insured as someone added to another party’s policy with “qualified or limited coverage,” while the named insured has broad rights.

Put plainly: the policy belongs to the named insured. Being added to it gives you a seat at a table someone else owns, on terms someone else negotiated.

What the certificate is

Your landlord’s certificate is a document about your landlord. It is a summary that says a policy exists, who the named insured is, and who may have been added. It does not replace the policy, and it does not usually change what the policy says. The wording of the policy and its endorsements is what actually decides how an additional insured is treated.

Where tenants get caught out

The policy is not yours to control. The named insured can change it, cancel it or let it lapse, and your position moves with it.

The limits are shared. Other people may be drawing on the same policy.

The scope can be narrow. Additional insured status is often tied to particular operations or premises, so it may not follow your business activity as a whole.

It does not speak to your own exposure. Your own operations, your own contents and your own people are separate questions that a landlord’s certificate does not answer.

What to check on your own paperwork

  1. Ask whether you are being listed on the landlord’s policy, or whether you are being asked to list the landlord on yours. The direction matters.
  2. Ask for the endorsement itself, not just the certificate, and read what it actually says.
  3. Read your lease for the insurance requirements it places on you.
  4. Compare any certificate against the declarations page of the policy it describes. Our guide to how to read a declarations page walks through where to look.
  5. Confirm you have a general liability policy of your own that names your business. General liability insurance in plain English explains what that policy is for, and our Florida general liability page covers the local picture.

The short version

Treat the landlord’s certificate as information, not protection. Whether your business is properly insured is a question for your own policy, read against your own lease and operations.

If you are not sure how the two fit together, that is a reasonable thing to ask a licensed agent to look at.
