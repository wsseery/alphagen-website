Cyber Insurance for Small Business in Florida: “We’re Too Small to Be a Target” Assumes Someone Chose You

Cyber insurance for small business in Florida starts with one assumption worth testing: that a person sat down and chose your company. Most of what reaches a small business did not choose anything. It is automated, it scans for an open door, and it does not read the sign on the door.

The most expensive sentence in small-business security

“We’re too small to be a target” assumes a human picked you out. Automated activity does not work that way. It looks for an open door and keeps looking.

The open doors are the ones the business needs

  • A contact form that accepts uploads.
  • A resume inbox.
  • An invoice attachment.
  • A shared folder a vendor can write to.

Every one of those exists because closing it would cost you customers, candidates or vendors. That is the real reason this is an insurance question and not only an IT question. Some of the exposure cannot be engineered away without shutting the door you opened on purpose.

Where it stops being an operations problem

If the only records you hold are your own, a breach is downtime, rebuilt systems and a week you do not get back. If you hold other people’s — customer payment details, patient files, employee records — it becomes a legal clock.

Florida’s data-breach statute requires notice to affected individuals no later than 30 days after the business determines a breach occurred, and notice to the Florida Department of Legal Affairs where 500 or more individuals in this state are affected (Fla. Stat. §501.171). Practices holding patient records generally carry further obligations under federal law — a question for your attorney, not your insurance agent.

What cyber liability is written around

Cyber liability is written around that sequence: the forensics, the notice, and the claims brought by the people whose data it was. Whether a given incident falls inside your policy is a question for that policy’s terms and exclusions.
