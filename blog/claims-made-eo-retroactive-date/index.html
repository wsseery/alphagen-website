Claims-Made E&O Retroactive Date: The Two Dates Most Firms Misunderstand

The claims-made E&O retroactive date is one of two dates that decide whether an errors and omissions policy responds, and only one of them is on the declarations page most people actually read. Most owners can tell you whether their E&O is claims-made. Far fewer can tell you what retroactive date is printed on it.

This is a plain-English walk through both dates, using the definitions published by two industry reference sources. It is general education, not a reading of anyone’s policy.

Date one: the claim, not the work

On a claims-made policy, the trigger is the claim. The IRMI glossary describes a claims-made policy as one that “provides coverage that is triggered when a claim is made against the insured during the policy period,” “regardless of when the wrongful act that gave rise to the claim took place.”

In other words, the date you did the work is not the date that matters first. The date the claim is made against you is.

Date two: the retroactive date

That is the general rule, and IRMI names its limit: “The one exception is when a retroactive date is applicable to a claims-made policy.” In that case, “the wrongful act that gave rise to the claim must have taken place on or after the retroactive date.”

So there are two dates working together. The claim has to be made during the policy period, and the work behind it has to fall on or after the retroactive date.

How a firm ends up uninsured while insured

Consider a firm that switches carriers. If the retroactive date resets to the new policy’s inception, every engagement completed before that date sits outside the policy the firm is paying for. The policy is live, the premium is being paid, and there can still be a gap behind it. Whether that applies to any given policy depends entirely on its actual terms, which is why the date is worth finding.

Why the endorsement list matters

The NAIC glossary of insurance terms defines an endorsement as “an amendment or rider to a policy adjusting the coverages and taking precedence over the general contract.” Retroactive dates and reporting provisions usually live in the endorsements, not on the summary page.

That is why the endorsement list deserves the same attention as the first page. We cover the same idea in more detail in our post on how to read a declarations page: the declarations page is the summary, and the forms and endorsements behind it can change what the policy says.

Two questions to ask about your own E&O

  1. Is your E&O claims-made? Most owners can answer this one.
  2. What retroactive date is printed on it? Most owners cannot, and the answer is usually found in the endorsements.

If you have changed carriers, ask whether the retroactive date moved when you did. Our page on professional liability insurance in Florida explains how this line is typically written for Florida firms.
