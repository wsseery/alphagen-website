Small Business Cyber Claim Florida: What It Actually Looks Like, Start to Finish

A small business cyber claim in Florida at a six-person professional firm is less dramatic than the headlines, and more expensive than owners expect, because most of the cost is not technical. It rarely starts with a ransom note. It starts with a bookkeeper mentioning that an invoice looked odd three weeks ago.

Here is the sequence, in the order a firm tends to meet it. It is a generic walk-through, not a description of any particular client or loss.

Day one: a question, not a repair

The first task is not fixing anything. It is answering two questions: did a breach occur, and whose records were in it. Answering them is digital forensics, billed by the hour, and a firm cannot give notice until they are answered.

Day two: a statute

Florida’s data-breach law, Fla. Stat. §501.171, requires notice to affected individuals no later than 30 days after the business determines that a breach occurred. It also requires notice to the Florida Department of Legal Affairs where 500 or more individuals in this state are affected. The clock is tied to the determination, which is why the forensic question on day one matters so much.

Day thirty: the mail

Notice means names and addresses, a letter that has to be legally sufficient, and a volume of calls nobody on a six-person team staffed for. None of that is a technical problem, and all of it takes time and money.

Then the claims

Claims are brought by the people whose data it was, against a firm whose insurance may or may not have been written for it. That last clause is the one worth sitting with.

A note on patient records

Firms holding patient records generally carry further federal obligations on top of all of this. That is a question for your attorney, not your insurance agent.

Where cyber liability fits

Cyber liability is the line written around that sequence. Whether any particular incident falls inside a given policy is a question for that policy’s terms and exclusions, which is the document worth reading before you need it. For the broader picture, see our overview of cyber insurance in Florida, and our earlier post on why a small business is not too small to be a target.

What to do with this

  1. Read the cyber policy’s terms and exclusions now, not during an incident.
  2. Walk the sequence above against your own firm: who would investigate, who would write the letters, who would answer the phones.
  3. Ask your attorney about any federal obligations tied to the records you hold.
