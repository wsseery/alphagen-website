Cyber Insurance for a Small Business in Florida: What a Six-Person Office Needs, and What It Can Skip

A six-person accounting office in Boca Raton does not think of itself as a cyber target. There is no server room, no IT department, and nothing a stranger would obviously want. What there is: a client list, bank details, and an email account that can authorise a wire transfer. That is the whole shopping list. Cyber insurance for a small business in Florida turns out to be less about hackers than about the clocks that start running the moment somebody realises something is wrong.

What actually happens

The version most small Florida offices meet is not ransomware. It is a quiet mailbox takeover.

Someone answers a convincing login prompt. For a few weeks nobody notices, while a forwarding rule copies everything out of the mailbox. Then an email that looks exactly like the usual one from the usual vendor arrives, with updated banking instructions, and someone in the office does their job and pays it.

Two separate problems start at once. The money is gone, which is a theft problem. And a stranger has been reading a mailbox full of other people’s personal information for a month, which is a notification problem — and in Florida the notification problem is the one with statutory deadlines attached.

Illustrative example. The businesses in this post, and the figures and timelines that describe them, are hypothetical, used to show how the rules apply. They are not statistics and not real clients. Statute figures are cited to their source.

Say the compromised mailbox held tax documents for 700 clients, and the firm works it out on a Tuesday. Under Florida’s breach statute the firm has, from the day it determines a breach occurred or has reason to believe one did, no later than 30 days to notify each affected person — and because 500 or more Floridians are involved, the same 30 days to notify the Department of Legal Affairs, with 15 extra days available only if good cause for the delay is put in writing. Above 1,000 people, the nationwide consumer reporting agencies have to be told as well.

Nobody in a six-person office has a drawer labelled “breach counsel.”

What cyber insurance for a small business in Florida does here

A cyber policy is really two policies stapled together, and it is worth knowing which half you are buying.

The first-party half is built to pay for your own costs: the forensic investigation that establishes what was actually taken, the lawyer who reads Fla. Stat. §501.171 for you, the notification letters, credit monitoring if you offer it, public relations, and lost income while you are not working. Most cyber policies address these as named breach-response costs rather than as general liability — which is why the wording matters more than the headline limit.

The third-party half responds when somebody else sues you, or a regulator asks questions. It is worth knowing that Florida’s breach statute does not itself create a private cause of action, and that the civil penalty for not notifying is capped at $500,000 — accruing at $1,000 a day for the first 30 days, then $50,000 for each subsequent 30-day period up to 180 days. That penalty applies per breach, not per person affected.

So the exposure for a small office is rarely a catastrophic lawsuit. It is a five-figure response bill, plus a statutory deadline you cannot negotiate, plus the possibility of a regulatory penalty if you miss it.

Where the gap usually sits

Here is the part that catches people, and it is the reason a six-person office can buy “cyber” and still find the thing that actually happened sitting outside it.

The stolen wire is often not addressed by the cyber part. Paying a fraudulent invoice because you were deceived is social engineering or funds transfer fraud. On most policies that is a separate endorsement with its own, much smaller sublimit — often a fraction of the policy limit — and sometimes it lives on a crime policy instead. A business can carry a $1,000,000 cyber limit and a $25,000 funds transfer sublimit and discover the difference on the worst possible day. Check your exclusions and sublimits page.

The cyber endorsement on a business owner’s policy is usually not a cyber policy. A business owner’s policy (BOP) often includes an “electronic data” or “data compromise” extension with a low sublimit and no breach-response team attached. It is better than nothing and it is not the same product.

Your vendor’s breach is your notification problem. If the firm that hosts your practice management software is breached, Florida gives that third-party agent 10 days to tell you — and then your 30-day clock to notify your clients is yours to run, not theirs. Most cyber policies address a vendor-side event only if dependent business interruption and contingent breach response are actually scheduled on the policy.

Business interruption has a waiting period. A wait of 8 or 12 hours before cover begins is common, and a mailbox compromise that never takes your systems offline may not trigger it at all.

What to check on your own policy

Five things, and all five are findable in about ten minutes with the declarations page and the schedule of endorsements in front of you.

  1. Find the social engineering or funds transfer fraud sublimit, as a number. If you cannot find one, assume it is not there.
  2. Check whether breach-response costs — forensics, legal, notification — sit inside the policy limit or outside it. Inside means a large notification eats the limit before any claim is made against you.
  3. Check the business interruption waiting period, and whether the trigger requires a system outage or merely a security failure.
  4. Check whether dependent or contingent coverage is scheduled, so a breach at a vendor who holds your client data is in scope.
  5. Check the retroactive date. A mailbox that was compromised before the policy started is a prior act, and prior acts are commonly excluded.
