Equipment breakdown coverage for a Florida business: the failure your property policy treats differently

A 48-unit building in Broward County loses its chiller in the first week of December. Nothing hit it. No storm, no fire, no break-in — a 19-year-old compressor simply stopped. The property manager calls the insurance agent expecting a claim, and gets a question back: was equipment breakdown on the policy, or wasn’t it? That question decides whether this is a covered loss or a special assessment, and most owners only learn which one they bought on the day the machine quits.

What actually happens when the machine stops

Illustrative example. The businesses in this post, and the figures and timelines that describe them, are hypothetical, used to show how the rules apply. They are not statistics and not real clients. Statute figures are cited to their source.

The chiller is dead on a Monday. The replacement unit is quoted at $82,000 with a ten-week lead time, and a temporary rental rig runs $4,100 a week to keep common areas and corridors habitable while everyone waits. By the time the new unit is set, the building has spent roughly $41,000 on rental equipment on top of the replacement.

Three different coverages could be in play and only one of them is designed for this. The commercial property policy responds to damage to property from an outside cause. The general liability policy responds to injury or damage the business causes someone else. Equipment breakdown — the modern name for what underwriters used to call boiler and machinery — is the one written for a machine that fails of its own accord: electrical arcing, mechanical breakdown, motor burnout, pressure-vessel rupture.

The distinction sounds academic until you read the property form’s own words. A commercial property policy is built around perils — things that happen to a building. A compressor that wears out is not a peril. It is the thing the building does for a living, doing it until it stops.

What equipment breakdown coverage for a Florida business actually does here

Equipment breakdown coverage is usually added to a commercial property policy by endorsement rather than bought as a separate policy, and it generally reaches three things: the damaged equipment itself, the damage that equipment does to the rest of the building when it fails, and the income or extra expense the failure causes while it is out of service. In the example above, that third bucket — the rental rig — is often larger than the repair.

Most equipment breakdown forms address electrical and mechanical failure, and most commercial property forms address storm, fire and impact. Neither is a substitute for the other, and the only place to confirm which one you hold is the endorsement schedule on your own policy. Spell it out with your agent: ask whether the policy carries an equipment breakdown endorsement, and what it lists as covered equipment.

One thing worth knowing before that call: Florida already inspects some of this machinery, and the inspection schedule is a reasonable proxy for how the state thinks about failure risk.

For boilers, the inspection rules are narrower than most owners assume. Florida provides that “the inspection requirements of this chapter apply only to boilers located in public assembly locations” (Fla. Stat. §554.108(1), 2026 Florida Statutes). Where they do apply, the intervals are set by boiler type: “power boilers and high pressure, high temperature water boilers shall receive a certificate inspection annually,” while “low pressure steam or vapor heating boilers shall receive a certificate inspection biennially” and “hot water heating boilers and hot water supply boilers shall receive a certificate inspection biennially” (§554.108(3)(a)–(c)). The certificate of operation that follows runs twelve months for a power boiler or high pressure, high temperature water boiler, and twenty-four months for heating and hot water supply boilers (§554.1101(2)).

Elevators are broader. Florida requires that “all elevators or other conveyances subject to this chapter must be annually inspected by a certified elevator inspector,” and the same paragraph provides that where a service maintenance contract applies, “an inspection is not required so long as the service contract remains in effect” (Fla. Stat. §399.061(1)(a), 2026 Florida Statutes).

Read those two schedules together and the shape of the risk is obvious. The state has decided this equipment needs looking at on a clock. An insurance policy written only around hurricanes and fires has no opinion about any of it.

Where the gap usually sits

The gap is almost never “we had no insurance.” It is that the building had a commercial property policy, assumed the machinery was inside it, and never read the endorsement schedule.

Three patterns show up most often in South Florida:

A policy with no equipment breakdown endorsement at all, on a building whose single most expensive component is a chiller. A policy that does carry the endorsement but schedules only the obvious units, leaving out pumps, elevator machine rooms, booster systems or the roof-mounted condensers added after the last renewal. And a policy where the endorsement exists, the equipment is scheduled, but the extra-expense limit is a fraction of what ten weeks of rented cooling costs in a Florida summer.

The second pattern is the one to look for this month, because it is created by the work you did during the year. Equipment added mid-term is not automatically scheduled equipment.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Find the endorsement schedule and look for equipment breakdown by name. It will not be in the declarations summary. If it is not on the schedule of forms and endorsements, it is not on the policy.
  2. Compare the scheduled equipment against what is actually on the roof and in the mechanical room. Anything installed or replaced since your last renewal — condensers, booster pumps, elevator controllers — needs to be reported.
  3. Read the extra-expense limit, not just the equipment limit. Ask what the form allows for temporary rental equipment and for how long, then compare it to a real rental quote for your largest unit.
  4. Pull the inspection records for anything the state inspects. For a boiler in a public assembly location, the certificate of operation carries its inspection date and its expiry; for an elevator, the annual inspection or the service maintenance contract is the record.
  5. Ask what the form excludes. Wear and tear, gradual deterioration and lack of maintenance are treated differently from a sudden breakdown on most forms, and the exclusions page is the only place that distinction is written down.

Multifamily and HOA insurance in Broward County

Multifamily buildings and homeowner and condominium associations in Broward County carry a risk profile that does not look much like a storefront’s: the most expensive thing on site is usually machinery, and the people who pay for a failure are the unit owners. Equipment breakdown, the extra-expense limit behind it, and the schedule of what is actually covered are worth a conversation before the renewal, not after the compressor.
