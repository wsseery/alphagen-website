A business owners policy: what it covers, and the three things it leaves out

A business owners policy — what it covers is usually described as “everything a small business needs,” and that is the problem. A BOP is a bundle, not a blanket. It packages the two policies almost every business buys anyway, at a better price than buying them separately, and then stops. The gaps it leaves are not obscure: for most Florida small businesses they are the three most likely sources of a serious claim.

A business owners policy — what it covers, bundled

Two policies, and usually a short list of extras.

Commercial general liability. The third-party half: bodily injury and property damage arising out of your premises and operations, plus the advertising and personal injury offences. This is the part that satisfies the insurance requirement in most commercial leases and most vendor contracts. If you want it in plain language, general liability in plain English walks through the form.

Commercial property. The first-party half: your building if you own it, your contents and equipment if you rent, and ordinarily some business income cover for the period you cannot trade after a covered loss. Limits, deductibles and the valuation basis — replacement cost or actual cash value — are all set on the declarations page, and they are where two BOPs at the same price stop being the same policy.

The extras vary by carrier, and they are usually modest sublimits rather than real coverage: some employee dishonesty, some accounts receivable, some signage, sometimes a small amount of data or equipment breakdown. Read the sublimits as the number, not the heading.

What makes a BOP attractive is that it is pre-packaged for low-hazard classes — professional offices, small retail, light service businesses — so underwriting is quick and the price reflects the bundle. What makes it risky is that the certificate it produces looks comprehensive.

The three things it leaves out

Workers’ compensation, and in Florida that is a statutory problem, not a preference. A BOP does not include workers’ compensation. Florida’s definition of covered employment reaches “all private employments in which four or more employees are employed by the same employer or, with respect to the construction industry, all private employment in which one or more employees are employed by the same employer” — Fla. Stat. §440.02(20)(b)2., 2024 Florida Statutes (including 2025C). So a four-person office and a one-person roofing business are both inside the requirement, and neither one’s BOP addresses it. This is the single most common gap, because the BOP arrives first and the fourth hire arrives later.

Professional liability. Claims that the work was wrong — the advice, the filing, the design, the treatment — sit outside general liability, which answers for bodily injury and property damage. Most general liability forms carry a professional services exclusion saying so. Any business whose product is its judgement needs this bought separately; the detail is on our law firm insurance in Boca Raton page, and it applies well beyond law firms.

Commercial auto. A personal auto policy generally excludes business use, and a BOP does not pick it up. One owned truck, one employee running deliveries in their own car, one trip to a job site — each is a different conversation, and hired and non-owned auto is the endorsement most small businesses have never heard of.

Beyond those three, the usual runners-up: flood and windstorm may be excluded or separately deductible depending on how the property is written, cyber is rarely more than a token sublimit if it appears at all, and umbrella sits above the BOP rather than inside it.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Count your employees against the four-employee threshold — or one, if you are in construction — and confirm whether you carry workers’ compensation at all.
  2. Read whether your property coverage is written at replacement cost or actual cash value, and check the limit against what it would cost to replace your contents today.
  3. Find every sublimit in the extras list and write the dollar figure next to the heading.
  4. Confirm whether anyone drives for the business, in any vehicle, and ask what your policy says about it.
  5. Ask directly whether professional liability is included. The answer is usually no, and it is better heard now than at claim time.

When the bundle is the right answer

For a low-hazard business with one location, no employees driving and no professional advice being sold, a BOP is genuinely efficient — two policies, one bill, one renewal date, one declarations page. The mistake is not buying one. The mistake is treating the bundle as the whole programme and discovering the edges during a claim.
