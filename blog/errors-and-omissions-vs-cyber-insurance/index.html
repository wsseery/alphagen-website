Errors and omissions vs cyber insurance: which one answers the claim?

Errors and omissions vs cyber insurance is a comparison most small professional offices run in the wrong order — after something has gone wrong, with two policies on the desk and a complaint that seems to fit both. The short version: errors and omissions answers for the work you did; cyber answers for the data you held. One event can produce both kinds of claim, and buying one does not quietly cover the other.

What actually happens

Illustrative example. The business in this post, and the figures and timelines that describe it, are hypothetical, used to show how the rules apply. They are not statistics and not real clients. Statute figures are cited to their source.

A six-person bookkeeping practice in Boca Raton has a bad fortnight. A mailbox is compromised, and a stranger reads client correspondence for two weeks before anyone notices — a data problem. In the same fortnight a reconciliation error in one client’s books survives two filing cycles, and the client’s lender finds it during a refinance — a work problem.

The practice has a cyber policy and no errors and omissions policy, because somebody decided a few years ago that cyber was the modern risk. The mailbox event is the kind of event a cyber policy’s breach-response section is written for. The reconciliation error is not in scope for anything the practice has bought.

What errors and omissions vs cyber insurance each respond to

The cleanest way to tell them apart is to ask what the complaint is about.

Errors and omissions — also called professional liability, and malpractice in the licensed trades — responds to an allegation that the service was performed negligently, or not performed. The injury is economic: a client lost money because of your advice, your filing, your calculation, your design. Most E&O policies address defence costs as well as settlement, and on a service-business claim the defence is frequently the larger number, because establishing that the work was reasonable takes expert time.

Cyber responds to a failure involving data and systems. Its first-party half addresses your own response costs — forensics, the lawyer who reads the breach statute, notification, lost income. Its third-party half responds when someone else sues you, or a regulator asks questions, over the data.

One structural point matters more than the wording. Both are usually claims-made, and the retroactive date is the trap. What matters on a claims-made policy is when the claim is made against you, and whether the policy was in force both then and when the work was done.

Florida’s limitations statute makes that concrete. An action for professional malpractice other than medical malpractice must be commenced within two years, and the statute provides that the period “shall run from the time the cause of action is discovered or should have been discovered with the exercise of due diligence,” limited to persons in privity with the professional — Fla. Stat. §95.11(5)(b), 2024 Florida Statutes (including 2025C). Two years from discovery, not from the invoice, is why the retroactive date on the declarations page is the most expensive thing to get wrong when switching carriers.

Where the gap usually sits

The cyber policy’s professional services exclusion. Many cyber forms exclude liability arising out of the rendering of professional services, precisely so they are not sold as E&O. A claim framed as “your mistake cost me money” meets that exclusion even if a computer was involved.

The E&O policy’s data exclusion. Running it the other way, plenty of E&O forms exclude breach-response costs and regulatory proceedings over personal data. Check your exclusions page before assuming one policy stretches.

The stolen wire belongs to neither, usually. Paying a fraudulent invoice because you were deceived is social engineering or funds transfer fraud — commonly a separate endorsement with its own much smaller sublimit, and sometimes on a crime policy instead.

One claim, two retentions. Where both policies do respond to one event, each brings its own retention and its own limit, and the two insurers will have a view about which goes first.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Find the professional services exclusion in your cyber policy, and the data or privacy exclusion in your E&O policy. Read both; they are usually endorsements rather than the main form.
  2. Find the retroactive date on each declarations page. If it matches the policy start date, earlier work is not in scope.
  3. Confirm whether defence costs sit inside or outside each limit of liability.
  4. Check the named insured line on both against every entity and trading name the business actually uses.
  5. Ask what the extended reporting period costs on each policy and how long you have to elect it, before you need to know.

Where this lands for a Boca Raton firm

An accounting, law, consulting or design practice is buying two different promises here, and the certificate on file rarely says which. The split is worked through for a law firm on our law firm insurance in Boca Raton page, and the cyber half on its own is in cyber insurance for a small Florida business.
