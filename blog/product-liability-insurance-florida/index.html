Product liability insurance in Florida: the clock that runs from the day you ship

If your business sells a physical thing — a sauce, a supplement, a bracket, a piece of equipment you assemble — the riskiest moment is not the sale. It is the years after it, when the thing is somewhere in Palm Beach or Broward County doing its job without you. Product liability insurance in Florida is meant to address that gap, and it is the part most small manufacturers and food producers assume they already have.

What actually happens after it leaves the building

Illustrative example. The businesses in this post, and the figures and timelines that describe them, are hypothetical, used to show how the rules apply. They are not statistics and not real clients. Statute figures are cited to their source.

A Boca Raton co-packer bottles a hot sauce under its own label and sells about 14,000 units a year through local groceries and restaurants. In year four, a case that shipped in year two is blamed for a batch of illnesses at one restaurant. The restaurant is sued; it names the co-packer; the co-packer's defence costs reach $70,000 before anyone decides whether the claim has merit.

Nothing in that sequence happened on the co-packer's premises. The exposure travelled with the product.

What product liability insurance in Florida actually does here

Here is the thing most owners get wrong, and it is good news: product liability is usually not a separate policy. It is a part of a general liability policy — the part underwriters call "products-completed operations," which responds to bodily injury or property damage arising out of a product after it has left your control, or out of work you finished.

That matters in two directions. A business with a general liability policy may already have the coverage and should check the limit rather than buy a second policy. And the products-completed operations aggregate is often a separate limit from the general aggregate, so the number that matters for a product claim may not be the one the owner remembers. Your own declarations page is where to confirm both.

The clock is longer than you think

The reason this coverage gets dropped at renewal is that owners quietly assume old inventory stops being their problem. Florida's limitations statute says otherwise.

Florida provides that "under no circumstances may a claimant commence an action for products liability, including a wrongful death action," "more than 12 years after delivery of the product to its first purchaser or lessee" (Fla. Stat. §95.031(2)(b), 2026 Florida Statutes). That twelve-year outer limit applies to products the statute presumes have "an expected useful life of 10 years or less," which is the default category — the same subsection sets a longer, twenty-year period for certain listed products and names categories, including improvements to real property, that it treats separately. The section is worth reading in full before anyone relies on a date.

Twelve years is the number to hold onto. A product sold in 2026 can produce a claim in the late 2030s, and the policy that answers it is the one in force when the claim is made or the injury occurs, depending on how the policy is written — not the one in force when you shipped the box.

Where the gap usually sits

Three places, in order of how often they appear. A business that stopped making a product and dropped the coverage, while what it already sold is still in service. A business whose general liability policy excludes products entirely — most often when a service business adds a small retail or packaged-goods line and nobody told the agent. And a business whose products-completed operations aggregate is far lower than its general aggregate, which only becomes visible when someone reads past the first page.

What to check on your own policy

  1. Find the products-completed operations aggregate on your declarations page and write it down next to the general aggregate. If they are different numbers, know which one a product claim uses.
  2. Check for a products exclusion. Read the schedule of forms and endorsements for anything excluding products or completed operations, and ask your agent directly whether your class is written with one.
  3. Tell your agent about every new physical item you sell — private-label goods, packaged food, merchandise, kits — even if it is a small share of revenue. A product line added mid-term is the most common source of an unexpected exclusion.
  4. Ask what happens if you stop selling it. If a product line is discontinued, ask how long the coverage needs to stay in place and what the options are, rather than letting it lapse at the next renewal.
  5. Keep batch, lot and ship-date records. A claim years later turns on when the product was delivered, and that date is the one Florida's twelve-year limit runs from.

Restaurant and food business insurance in Boca Raton

Restaurants, co-packers and specialty food producers in Boca Raton sit on both sides of this: they sell a physical product and they are the buyer named when somebody else's product goes wrong. The products-completed operations limit, and whether anything on the policy excludes it, is a short conversation with a long tail.
