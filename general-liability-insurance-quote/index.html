What general liability insurance is written to address

Commercial general liability (GL) is usually the first policy a Florida business is asked to show. It is written for claims brought by third parties, such as customers, visitors, neighbors or another contractor on a job, that allege bodily injury or property damage arising from your premises, your operations or your products. Most forms also include personal and advertising injury, and the insurer’s duty to defend a suit the policy applies to.

GL is generally not where other exposures sit. Injuries to your own employees are a workers’ compensation matter; damage to your own building and equipment is commercial property; professional mistakes, vehicles and, for most businesses that serve alcohol, liquor liability are written separately. Whether a particular claim falls inside a particular policy depends on that policy’s insuring agreement, exclusions and endorsements. For a worked example of where the edges are, see what a Florida restaurant’s general liability policy actually excludes.

Who needs general liability in Florida

For most businesses the requirement comes from a contract rather than a statute. A commercial lease, a client’s vendor agreement, a general contractor’s subcontract or a venue permit will typically name the limits it expects and ask for a certificate of insurance, often with that party added as an additional insured. Read those clauses before you buy, because they decide the limits and endorsements you need.

Contractors are the clearest case where Florida regulation itself asks for liability insurance. The Construction Industry Licensing Board’s rule requires certified and registered contractors to obtain and continually maintain public liability and property damage insurance, with minimum amounts set by license category, and allows the Board to audit licensees for proof of coverage (Fla. Admin. Code R. 61G4-15.003). Your license category, not your trade name, decides which minimum applies.

Florida rules that shape a liability claim

Florida apportions fault. In a negligence action, the court enters judgment against each party according to its percentage of fault “and not on the basis of the doctrine of joint and several liability.” Under the statute as amended in 2023, a party found to be “greater than 50 percent at fault for his or her own harm may not recover any damages,” outside medical-negligence cases (Fla. Stat. §768.81).

Two practical consequences for a business owner:

  • Your records are part of your defense. Inspection logs, incident reports, signed contracts and certificates collected from subcontractors are what fault gets argued with. A business that can show what it did tends to be easier to defend, and easier to underwrite.
  • Contracts can move liability back onto you. An indemnity or hold-harmless clause can make you answer for losses the statute would otherwise apportion to someone else. Whether a GL policy responds to that contractual liability depends on its wording, so check both the contract and the policy before you sign.

What drives the cost of a general liability policy

There is no single price for general liability. Carriers rate each business on its own exposure, and the factors that typically move a quote are:

  • What you do. The class of business and the operations you actually perform, including any work you subcontract.
  • How large the exposure is. Depending on the class, carriers commonly rate on payroll, revenue or square footage.
  • Where you operate. Your premises, the number of locations and the counties you work in.
  • Your claims history. Prior claims and how they were resolved.
  • The terms you need. Limits, deductible, and contract-driven endorsements such as additional insured, waiver of subrogation or primary and noncontributory wording.
  • How you manage subcontractors. Whether you collect their certificates and are named as an additional insured on their policies.

Because carriers weigh these factors differently, terms for similar-looking businesses can vary from one carrier to the next. As an independent agency, AlphaGen can request terms from more than one carrier in a single process.

What to have ready

  • A plain description of what the business does, and any work you subcontract.
  • Estimated annual revenue and payroll.
  • Your locations and the counties you work in.
  • Your current policy, renewal date and claims history (loss runs), if you have them.
  • Any contract or lease that sets the limits or endorsements you must carry.
